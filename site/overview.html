What ARALD is, and why

A network for places the internet doesn't reach

Most communication tools assume you have internet. ARALD doesn't — devices pass content, messages, and services directly to each other, hopping from one to the next until they reach whoever needs them.

If a place has no, intermittent, expensive, or unreliable connectivity, it's a candidate for ARALD. No cell towers, no Wi-Fi router, no satellite link required — and nothing needs to be online at once: if nobody nearby can answer a request right now, it waits for the next device that can carry it forward.

Ten environments, one architecture

Where it works

The first three drove the design — equally important, not a hierarchy. The same devices and code apply, unchanged, everywhere else on this list.

Mountain shelters

Remote huts where cell coverage never arrives and running a cable isn't an option — the scenario that has driven ARALD's design from the start.

Emergency & disaster response

Earthquakes, floods, fires — exactly when normal networks are damaged, overloaded, or without power, and coordination matters most.

NGOs & humanitarian operations

Field bases, mobile clinics, and temporary camps in areas without reliable infrastructure.

Ships, coasts & islands

A boat becomes a relay, carrying messages between harbors it passes.

Expeditions & remote stations

Weeks without connectivity, planned for rather than treated as an outage.

Rural & isolated communities

Villages without stable cellular coverage, connected device to device.

Forests & nature reserves

Large natural areas where responders and rangers can't rely on a signal.

Crowded events

Here the mesh doesn't replace connectivity — it multiplies it at scale.

Schools

A static local library — encyclopedias, courses — reachable without live internet.

Temporary & crisis infrastructure

Construction sites, relief operations, any network stood up quickly with no time to plan cabling.
